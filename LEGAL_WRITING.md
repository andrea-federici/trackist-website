# How to write the Terms of Use and Privacy Policy

Write for an athlete opening either page for the first time. Use the voice of the [Terms of Use](terms.html) in the [Privacy Policy](privacy.html) too. Address the reader as “you”. Identify the provider in the unnumbered opening so the reader knows who “we” means.

## Name the thing you mean

An earlier draft said, “You need an account to use the diary.” Andrea’s correction was **“You need an account to use StrideBuddy.”** The account requirement applies to the whole app. Someone reading it for the first time has no reason to know what “the diary” means.

Do not write “diary” or “training diary” in either document. Andrea rejected it twice on 2026-11-24. Say what the reader added instead: “the workouts and races you log”, “your training data” or “your entries”. Use **StrideBuddy** for the product.

Apply the same care to other feature names. StrideBuddy suggests a training week. The athlete opens Plan to see it. Quote the local app label when telling someone where to go, and only after checking that the control exists. A 2026-11-23 draft said “On iPhone you can remove the downloaded data in Settings.” No such control is in Settings: signing out removes the copy, so the policy now says “Signing out removes that copy.”

## Write the Terms around decisions and responsibilities

Say who can do something, who must do it and under what conditions. The sentence “You decide whether to do each suggested session and how to do it” tells an athlete exactly which decision is theirs. Use “must” for a requirement and “may” for a permission or possibility. When a right, exception or limit qualifies a rule, keep it beside that rule.

Use complete sentences in the body. A short heading such as “Your account” can stand on its own. Under it, write “You need an account to use StrideBuddy.” Give a second action or exception its own sentence if that makes it easier to follow.

## Write the Privacy Policy around what happens to someone’s data

Start with the action the reader knows, such as logging a workout, sending a photo or turning off a setting. Explain which information is involved, who receives it, why, and what the reader can control. Where retention or deletion matters, say how long a copy lasts or what determines when it is removed.

**Open with who is responsible, what the policy covers and the few promises a reader cares about most.** A 2026-11-23 draft opened with four unrelated facts: what StrideBuddy saves, where you review it, that entries still sync with AI off, and that analytics is a separate choice. Andrea asked what those facts were doing at the top. The opening now names the controller and the scope, then states the commitments people look for first: we do not sell data or use it for advertising, nobody trains AI models on it, and analytics stays off unless you turn it on.

**Describe data as categories with “such as”, and never let a partial list read as complete.** “StrideBuddy saves the workouts and races you log in your account” reads as if nothing else is saved. Write “We store what you add to StrideBuddy, such as your athlete profile, the workouts and races you log, your notes and your plans.” Then name the other kinds separately: settings, accepted terms and the devices on the account.

**Test every sentence with one question: does it tell the reader who gets their data, why, for how long, or what they can do about it?** If it does none of these, cut it. Andrea rejected these on 2026-11-24, and they are the pattern to avoid:

- “You can leave notes blank.” The reader can leave most fields blank, and nothing about their data follows from it.
- “Someone using the same device may still see recent searches or unfinished entries or feedback.” It describes what is on a device, not what we do with data.
- “During Workout Mode, the session title can appear on your lock screen.” It is ordinary iPhone behavior, not a disclosure.
- Implementation detail, such as what the app does when it cannot load a setting, or that an offline change reaches the server after reconnecting.
- A provider’s own subcontractor, such as the spam checker behind the email service. Name the providers we chose, not theirs.

- How we run our own inbox, such as which mail service receives support email or where it forwards. Say what kind of recipient holds the data, for example “our email provider”, and only where a reader needs it.
- A sentence that only says what the reader does with their own copy, such as where a shared image goes after they send it.
- A deadline the law already sets for us, such as answering a request within one month.

A privacy review, impact assessment or audit finds many true facts. The policy does not repeat them. Those findings stay in the review, and the policy describes what happens to the reader’s data.

**Name a sensitive category once, in the place where the law needs it.** A 2026-11-23 draft said “If you write about an illness or injury, those details sync to your account like any other entry.” Listing topics a person might write about has no end. The policy names the category, health information, once, in the legal-basis section, with one example and what the reader controls.

**Do not write a number that someone else controls.** A provider’s retention period changes without telling us. Write “OpenAI and Google may keep requests for a limited time to detect abuse” and link to their own pages. Write our own periods only where they are settled, and use the rule instead of the number when the number is about to change, as with backups that “are replaced automatically”.

**Do not promise a limit Andrea has not decided.** A 2026-11-24 draft said “We do not use them to make decisions about you” about race predictions. Andrea may want the estimates to improve later predictions and plans, so the promise would bind the product for no reason. Write what we do, and add a restriction only when Andrea has chosen it or the law requires it.

**Say what the estimate covers, not one example as if it were all.** “Race predictions and the VO₂max estimate” reads as if those are the only estimates. Write “your race times and measures such as your VO₂max and your running qualities”.

**Write each legal basis as a purpose followed by its basis.** “We use iPhone usage statistics only with your consent” reads as a fact, not a reason. Write “To learn how people use the iPhone app, we rely on your consent.”

Keep a technical detail when removing it would change someone’s understanding of what happens. A recipient’s name, a retention period, or a difference between deleting an entry and deleting a backup can matter. Name each provider once, with its job and where it processes data, in the section about recipients. Check each claim against the released behavior it describes. Keep disclosures for an unreleased feature with its branch until it ships. Describe current processing. A correction about what an earlier version did belongs in the notice to existing users, not in the policy. Other apps’ policies can suggest a clearer way to explain something. StrideBuddy’s own practices determine what we say.

## Write each language for its reader

Settle the meaning in English first. Write natural Italian, Spanish and French using the familiar singular form used in the app. Each version carries equal weight. Write each language as if it were the original. A translator can change the order of a sentence, split it in two or choose a different construction, as long as the content stays exactly the same. A word-for-word copy of the English reads badly in Italian, Spanish and French.

**Every sentence names its subject, in every language.** On 2026-11-24 Andrea rejected “Riguarda l’app StrideBuddy per iPhone, l’app web su web.stridebuddy.app e questo sito.” because nothing says what “riguarda”. Write “Questa informativa sulla privacy vale per l’app StrideBuddy per iPhone, per l’app web e per questo sito.” The same applies to English: “It covers…” after a sentence about the policy points back, so write “This privacy policy applies to…”. In Italian and Spanish, where the verb can stand alone, make the subject explicit: name StrideBuddy when the service does something, write “noi” or “nosotros” when Andrea does, and use a noun subject such as “la base giuridica è…” in place of a bare verb.

**Never pack a list and a condition into one sentence.** Andrea also rejected “Riceviamo il tuo indirizzo email, il tuo nome se scegli di condividerlo e gli identificativi…”. Give each item with its own condition a sentence of its own: “StrideBuddy riceve il tuo indirizzo email. StrideBuddy riceve anche il tuo nome, se scegli di condividerlo.”

| English | Italian | Spanish | French |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| You need an account to use StrideBuddy. | Per usare StrideBuddy ti serve un account. | Necesitas una cuenta para usar StrideBuddy. | Tu as besoin d’un compte pour utiliser StrideBuddy. |

Read each paragraph on its own in that language. Can a reader tell who takes each action? Then compare the legal meaning across versions. All four must preserve the same rights, obligations, choices, exceptions, named recipients, periods and links. Keep the update date first, followed by the opening and the same numbered sections in the same order. Make the four language changes together.

## Keep this guide current

When Andrea corrects wording in either document, fix similar passages and their translations. If the correction teaches something another editor could use, update this guide in the same change. Put the example under the rule it illustrates. Quote the earlier and preferred wording when the distinction matters, then explain in one sentence when to apply it. If the preference changes, revise the existing example so the next editor has one clear instruction. A change to a provider, date or product fact belongs in the policy itself unless it also teaches a writing preference.
